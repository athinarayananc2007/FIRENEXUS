# FIRENEXUS Statistical Analysis & Anomaly Detection Methodology

## 1. Executive Summary & Scientific Scope

The FIRENEXUS statistical calibration and anomaly detection engine provides rigorous mathematical analysis of satellite active fire observations derived from NASA FIRMS MODIS (Terra/Aqua) and VIIRS (Suomi-NPP, NOAA-20, NOAA-21) sensors.

> **Scientific Humility & Validation Disclaimer:**
> Algorithmic calibrations and statistical metrics generated by FIRENEXUS are data-driven statistical models derived from coincident satellite observations. They do **not** constitute ground-truth active fire energy measurements or airborne radiometer cross-validation. Software test suites confirm code correctness and mathematical integrity under synthetic and historical datasets; they do **not** replace peer-reviewed physical sensor intercalibration.

---

## 2. Theoretical Basis for Cross-Sensor FRP Comparison

Fire Radiative Power (FRP, measured in Megawatts, $\text{MW}$) quantifies the rate of radiant energy emitted by an active fire cluster. It is calculated using the middle-infrared (MIR) radiance anomaly method (Wooster et al., 2003) or the bi-spectral method (Dozier, 1981):

$$FRP = \frac{A_{\text{pix}} \cdot \sigma}{a} \left(L_{\text{MIR}} - L_{\text{MIR,bg}}\right)$$

Where:
- $A_{\text{pix}}$ is the pixel ground footprint area.
- $\sigma$ is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant ($5.6704 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{W}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}\cdot\text{K}^{-4}$).
- $a$ is an empirical sensor-specific coefficient.
- $L_{\text{MIR}}$ and $L_{\text{MIR,bg}}$ are the pixel and background MIR spectral radiances.

### Resolution Discrepancy & Scaling
- **MODIS**: $1\text{ km} \times 1\text{ km}$ footprint at nadir, expanding up to $\sim 4.8\text{ km} \times 2.0\text{ km}$ at scan edges (factor of $\approx 10$ area expansion).
- **VIIRS**: $375\text{ m} \times 375\text{ m}$ footprint at nadir with pixel aggregation smoothing across scan swaths (footprint remains $< 0.8\text{ km} \times 0.8\text{ km}$ at scan edge).

Because VIIRS samples with $\sim 7\times$ to $10\times$ smaller pixel surface area than MODIS at nadir, VIIRS can detect much smaller sub-pixel fires and lower total FRP thresholds ($\sim 0.5\text{–}1.0\text{ MW}$) compared to MODIS ($\sim 5\text{–}10\text{ MW}$). When aggregating co-incident clusters into harmonized fire events, paired FRP comparisons must account for:
1. Spatial aggregation over matching spatial envelopes (coincident radius $\le 1.5\text{ km}$ or event boundaries).
2. Point Spread Function (PSF) integration over pixel footprints.
3. Triangular scan angle weighting.

## 3. Mathematical Formulas

### 3.1 Univariate Summary Metrics
For a sample of $N$ observations $X = \{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_N\}$:
- **Sample Mean ($\bar{x}$):**
  $$\bar{x} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N x_i$$
- **Sample Standard Deviation ($s$):**
  $$s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x})^2}$$
- **Sample Variance ($s^2$):**
  $$s^2 = \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x})^2$$
- **Sample Skewness ($g_1$):**
  $$g_1 = \frac{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x})^3}{\left(\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x})^2\right)^{3/2}}$$
- **Sample Kurtosis ($a_4$ - Fisher excess):**
  $$a_4 = \frac{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x})^4}{\left(\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x})^2\right)^2} - 3$$
- **Percentiles ($P_p$):**
  Computed via linear interpolation of rank $r = 1 + (N - 1) \cdot \frac{p}{100}$ on sorted values.

### 3.2 Paired Bivariate Statistics & Error Metrics
For paired observations $(x_i, y_i)$ where $x_i$ is MODIS FRP and $y_i$ is VIIRS FRP:
- **Pearson Product-Moment Correlation ($r$):**
  $$r = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x})^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^N (y_i - \bar{y})^2}}$$
- **Spearman Rank Correlation ($\rho$):**
  Computed as the Pearson correlation of fractional ranks $R(x_i)$ and $R(y_i)$:
  $$\rho = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N (R(x_i) - \bar{R}_x)(R(y_i) - \bar{R}_y)}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^N (R(x_i) - \bar{R}_x)^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^N (R(y_i) - \bar{R}_y)^2}}$$
- **Mean Bias Error (MBE):**
  $$\text{MBE} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (y_i - x_i)$$
- **Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE):**
  $$\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (y_i - x_i)^2}$$
- **Mean Absolute Error (MAE):**
  $$\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N |y_i - x_i|$$
- **Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE):**
  $$\text{MAPE} = \frac{100\%}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left| \frac{y_i - x_i}{x_i} \right| \quad (x_i \ne 0)$$

## 4. Statistical Calibration Models

Calibration models estimate equivalent MODIS FRP from VIIRS observations or vice-versa to allow seamless multi-decadal climate analysis.

### 4.1 Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) Linear Regression
$$y = \beta_1 x + \beta_0 + \epsilon$$
The parameters minimizing sum of squared residuals $\sum_{i=1}^N (y_i - (\beta_1 x_i + \beta_0))^2$ are:
$$\beta_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x})^2} = \frac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{\text{Var}(X)}$$
$$\beta_0 = \bar{y} - \beta_1 \bar{x}$$

### 4.2 Zero-Intercept Ratio Model (Direct Scaling)
Because zero fire radiance physically corresponds to zero radiative power ($FRP = 0 \implies \hat{y} = 0$), forcing the regression intercept through the origin is physically sound:
$$y = \beta x + \epsilon$$
The optimal slope coefficient $\beta$ is:
$$\beta = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N x_i y_i}{\sum_{i=1}^N x_i^2}$$

### 4.3 Goodness of Fit & Test Evaluation
- **Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$):**
  $$R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^N (y_i - \bar{y})^2} = 1 - \frac{SS_{\text{res}}}{SS_{\text{tot}}}$$
- **Test Set Metrics:**
  - $R^2_{\text{test}}$
  - $\text{RMSE}_{\text{test}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{\text{test}}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{test}}} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}$
  - $\text{MAE}_{\text{test}} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{test}}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{test}}} |y_i - \hat{y}_i|$
  - $\text{Mean Bias}_{\text{test}} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{test}}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{test}}} (\hat{y}_i - y_i)$

### 4.4 Data Leakage Prevention (Chronological Splitting)
Active fire activity exhibits strong autocorrelation and temporal clustering. Standard random train/test splits lead to severe data leakage, where observations from the same fire event on the same day appear in both training and test sets, artificially inflating $R^2$.
- **Enforced Strategy:** Chronological split based on acquisition timestamp:
  $$\max_{i \in \text{Train}}(t_i) \le \min_{j \in \text{Test}}(t_j)$$
  All training observations strictly precede all test observations in time.

## 5. Anomaly Detection Engine

The anomaly detection engine identifies statistical outliers, physical instrument anomalies, and spatially isolated candidates without modifying raw data.

### 5.1 Parametric Z-Score
Detects values departing significantly from the sample mean:
$$Z_i = \frac{x_i - \bar{x}}{s}$$
- **Threshold:** Flagged if $|Z_i| > 3.0$ (exceeds $3\sigma$).

### 5.2 Modified Z-Score (Median Absolute Deviation - MAD)
Parametric Z-scores are vulnerable to masking when extreme outliers pull the sample mean and inflate the standard deviation. The Modified Z-score uses robust location and dispersion estimators:
$$\text{MAD} = \text{median}\left(\{|x_i - \tilde{x}|\}_{i=1}^N\right) \quad \text{where } \tilde{x} = \text{median}(X)$$
$$M_i = \frac{0.6745 \cdot (x_i - \tilde{x})}{\text{MAD}}$$
- **Threshold:** Flagged if $|M_i| > 3.5$ (Boris Iglewicz and David Hoaglin, 1993).
- If $\text{MAD} = 0$, falls back to mean absolute deviation from median.

### 5.3 Interquartile Range (IQR) Upper Fence
Detects distribution tail extremes:
$$\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1 = P_{75} - P_{25}$$
$$\text{Upper Fence} = Q_3 + 1.5 \times \text{IQR}$$
$$\text{Extreme Fence} = Q_3 + 3.0 \times \text{IQR}$$
- **Threshold:** Flagged as `extreme_frp` if $x_i > \text{Upper Fence}$ (or Extreme Fence).

### 5.4 Physical Instrument Saturation
- **MODIS Band 21 / 22:** Saturation limit at $\ge 500.0\text{ K}$ brightness temperature.
- **VIIRS I4 Band ($3.74\ \mu\text{m}$):** Saturation limit at $\ge 367.0\text{ K}$.
Observations at or above these thresholds are flagged as `saturation` anomalies, indicating that radiative energy may be clipped or lower-bounded by detector dynamics.

### 5.5 Spatial Isolation Test
Active wildfires typically occur in clusters. A solitary hotspot detected far from any other fire detection during the temporal window is evaluated for false-alarm probability (e.g., glint, gas flare, or noise artifact):
- Minimum distance to nearest neighbor:
  $$d_{\min}(i) = \min_{j \ne i} \text{Haversine}(\text{lat}_i, \text{lon}_i, \text{lat}_j, \text{lon}_j)$$
- **Threshold:** Flagged as `isolated_hotspot` if $d_{\min}(i) > 15.0\text{ km}$ ($15,000\text{ meters}$).

## 6. Diurnal Analysis & Sampling Limitations

The Diurnal Fire Activity Analysis tracks the hourly UTC distribution and sensor-specific sampling characteristics.

### 6.1 Polar Sun-Synchronous Orbit Sampling
MODIS and VIIRS operate on sun-synchronous low Earth orbits (LEO) with nominal orbital altitude $\sim 700\text{–}825\text{ km}$:
- **Terra (MODIS):** Equatorial crossing at $\sim 10:30\text{ AM}$ (descending) and $\sim 10:30\text{ PM}$ (ascending) local solar time.
- **Aqua (MODIS):** Equatorial crossing at $\sim 1:30\text{ PM}$ (ascending) and $\sim 1:30\text{ AM}$ (descending) local solar time.
- **Suomi-NPP & NOAA-20/21 (VIIRS):** Equatorial crossing at $\sim 1:30\text{ PM}$ (ascending) and $\sim 1:30\text{ AM}$ (descending) local solar time.

### 6.2 Scientific Limitations of LEO Diurnal Curves
1. **Sampling Bias:** LEO satellites only observe a given point on Earth approximately 2 to 4 times per 24-hour cycle. They miss the diurnal peak if it occurs between overpass intervals (e.g., peak fire intensity often occurs at 3:00–5:00 PM local solar time).
2. **Cloud Cover & Smoke Obscuration:** Optical and infrared active fire detection cannot penetrate thick cloud decks or pyrocumulonimbus clouds. Cloudy days artificially reduce detected fire frequency.
3. **Scan Angle & Edge Distortion:** Off-nadir observations cover larger ground footprints, altering detection sensitivity.
4. **Geostationary Complementarity:** For continuous 5-15 minute diurnal tracking, geostationary sensors (GOES ABI, Meteosat SEVIRI, Himawari AHI) must complement LEO observations.
